Deep dive: how a hidden fault shows up in these data

The competition's inputs are potential-field and topographic transforms of the GeoDAWN survey plus a regional geodetic strain model. Reading them as physical instruments rather than as image bands is what the five hypotheses rest on.

  • Magnetics see the basement, not the surface. An aeromagnetic survey measures the field of magnetised crust; a fault that offsets that crust under alluvium produces a linear gradient edge and a shallow equivalent-source depth. Reduction-to-pole (rtp) centres an anomaly over its source, horizontal and vertical gradients (tmi_hg, tmi_vg) turn an offset into a crest line, and Euler deconvolution (deq_n100a15, ieq_n100a15) estimates how deep that edge is. That triple — edge, crest, depth — is the whole of Hypothesis 1, and the leaderboard agrees: the validated ridge loads positively on the magnetic-edge features and negatively on Euler depth, i.e. the winning submissions sat on shallow magnetic edges.
  • Gravity sees mass where magnetics sees nothing. Radiometric and magnetic response both collapse under young, weakly magnetised volcanic cover, whereas density contrast survives. The second invariant of the gravity gradient tensor (geod_2ndinv) is rotation-invariant, so a fault is detected at any strike — a property that single component maps (and every previous construction in the family) lack. That is Hypothesis 2.
  • Topography sees the scarp, but only at the right resolution. A Quaternary fault scarp is a 1–3 m step; the competition grid is 100 m. Detrending at 1 m and skeletonising recovers traces that are geometrically destroyed at the competition resolution. This is the strongest known source of post-publication fault discoveries, and the honest blocker is that a single lidar quadrangle exceeds this sandbox's 3 GB of RAM. That is Hypothesis 3, ranked last on cost and first on ceiling.
  • Strain models see kinematics, not geology. geod_shearrate and geod_dilaterate are model outputs, so a fault that is invisible to mapping but occupies a kinematic node still has an expression there. Hypothesis 4 turns that into a node detector instead of another layer to thin.
  • The catalogue's own geometry is evidence. Stepovers and relay ramps are places where a structure must continue across a gap; if the connecting trace is buried, the compiler leaves exactly the hole the scorer is asking you to fill. That is Hypothesis 5, and it is cheap because it needs no geophysics at all.

Two physics-level caveats that constrain any detector here. First, all of these fields are finite-bandwidth: at 100 m sampling, a fault's signature is at most a few cells wide, so the metric's 300 m kernel is wider than the target — which is why a single well-placed dot can earn credit from several hidden truth pixels at once, and why precision beats recall (see Method §2). Second, the scorer masks the known catalogue, so a perfect reproductions of the mapped geology scores zero. The signal that matters lives in the residual, exactly where the geophysics is weakest.

How to re-check any claim on this site

  1. Open the JSON receipt named next to the claim (all of them are in data/ and mirrored in docs/downloads/).
  2. Run the script that produced it — the mapping is in Evidence §6.
  3. For any external claim, follow the link above and compare the sentence you are checking with the sentence on the page. Where the status is carried, treat it as unverified until you have done so.
